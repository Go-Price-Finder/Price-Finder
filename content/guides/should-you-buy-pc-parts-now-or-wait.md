# Should You Buy PC Parts Now or Wait?

Almost every buying guide on the internet gives the same advice: wait. Wait for
Black Friday, wait for Prime Day, wait for the next generation to launch and
push last year's parts down. It is good advice most of the time, because most
of the time computer parts get cheaper as they age.

2026 is not most of the time. For one large category of components — memory and
storage — prices have been going *up* all year, and the seasonal advice that
works in a normal year will cost you money in this one.

This guide is about telling those two situations apart.

## What is actually happening to prices

Memory is in the middle of the sharpest price rise the PC market has seen in
over a decade, and the cause is not gamers or builders. It is AI infrastructure.
Data-centre operators are buying DRAM and NAND flash in enormous volumes on
long-term contracts, and consumer parts compete for the same fabrication
capacity.

The numbers are stark. DRAM and NAND contract prices rose roughly 60% in a
single quarter in Q2 2026. TrendForce's Q3 2026 forecast has DRAM climbing a
further 13–18% quarter-over-quarter and NAND flash a further 10–15%. That is
described in the trade press as a *slowdown*, which tells you how steep the
prior quarter was. Analysts covering the sector are explicit that meaningful
price relief is "still some way off," because AI capacity remains the industry's
priority allocation.

This spills into things that are not obviously memory. A graphics card is, from
a bill-of-materials perspective, substantially a memory product. When DRAM
doubles, board partners either raise prices or cut margin, and in 2026 they have
largely raised prices. The same is true of pre-built systems, mini PCs, NAS
enclosures sold with drives included, and laptops.

## The practical split

The question "should I buy now or wait?" has two different answers in 2026
depending on what you are buying, and the dividing line is whether the part's
cost is dominated by memory.

**Buy now, if you need it:** RAM kits, SSDs, NVMe drives, anything sold with
storage included, and to a lesser degree graphics cards. These are the parts
where waiting has been the losing move all year. If a 32GB DDR5 kit is on your
list and the price looks high, the uncomfortable truth is that today's high
price has a reasonable chance of being next quarter's bargain. Waiting for a
sale on a rising market means paying a discount off a higher list price, which
is often worse than paying full price today.

**Waiting still works:** cases, power supplies, coolers, fans, motherboards,
peripherals, monitors, and CPUs. None of these are memory-dominated. They
follow the ordinary pattern — new generation launches, previous generation
discounts, seasonal sales are real sales. The classic calendar advice applies
here essentially unchanged.

That split is the single most useful thing to take away. It is also why generic
"best time to buy PC parts" articles are unusually unreliable right now: they
were written against a market where everything drifts downward, and half the
market currently doesn't.

## How to tell a real discount from a marked-up one

This matters more when prices are rising, because rising list prices make
discounts look bigger without making them better.

The number that matters is not the percentage off. It is the price you would
actually pay, compared with what that same part actually sold for recently.
A retailer that raises a drive from $89 to $119 and then advertises "20% off"
at $95 is showing you a genuine markdown against its own current list price and
a 7% increase against what you could have paid a month ago. Both statements are
true. Only one of them is about your money.

Three habits are worth more than any single tool here. Check the price against
more than one seller, because in a supply-constrained market the spread between
retailers widens considerably — parts that used to sit within a few dollars of
each other can differ by a third. Note the capacity and the exact model number
rather than the product family, because manufacturers quietly introduce
lower-specification variants at the old price point when costs rise. And treat
urgency language as noise: "limited stock" has been true of memory for most of
2026 and therefore tells you nothing about whether today's price is good.

## What a launch actually does to prices

The most reliable price movement in components is not seasonal, it is
generational. When a new CPU or GPU generation ships, the previous generation
gets cheaper — sometimes weeks *before* the launch, as retailers clear
inventory ahead of it.

This still works in 2026, with one caveat: it works on the silicon, not on the
memory attached to it. A previous-generation graphics card will come down
relative to its launch price, but its floor is being propped up by what the
memory on it costs. Expect smaller clearance discounts than you would have seen
in 2023 or 2024, and expect them to disappear faster.

For CPUs specifically, generational clearance remains one of the best-value
moments in the entire calendar, because a CPU's price is not memory-linked.

## The honest limits of this advice

Two things are worth saying plainly.

First, nobody knows when the memory market turns. The forecasts above are
quarterly projections from a market-research firm, not guarantees, and the
sector has surprised in both directions before. What we can say with confidence
is the direction of the last four quarters and the direction of the current
published forecast. What we cannot say is which month it reverses.

Second, "buy now" is not the same as "buy anything." If a build can wait a year
entirely — if the machine you have still does the job — then waiting a year is
a different decision from waiting a quarter, and this guide has nothing useful
to say about it. The advice here is narrow: if you have decided to buy in the
next few months and you are choosing *when* within that window, memory-heavy
parts argue for sooner and everything else argues for the usual patience.
